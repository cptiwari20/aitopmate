Most co-founder searches fail in the same way. Someone writes a thoughtful post — "Looking for a technical co-founder for an AI startup in [space]" — gets forty replies, has twelve coffee chats, and ends up with a spreadsheet of nice people and no partner.

The problem isn't a lack of people. It's that a co-founder relationship is closer to a marriage than a hire, and you can't find a marriage through a spreadsheet.

Here's what we've seen work for founders in our community, and what reliably doesn't.

## First, be honest about what you need

"Technical co-founder" used to mean one thing: someone who could build the product. In an AI company in 2026, that's shifted. A lot of the prototype can be built by a determined non-engineer with good tools. What you usually need now is one of these:

- **Someone who can make it reliable.** Evals, data pipelines, cost control, the unglamorous work of making an AI product behave the same way on Tuesday as it did on Monday.
- **Someone who can sell into a nervous buyer.** Enterprise customers are more cautious about AI, not less. Somebody who can navigate security reviews and anxious stakeholders is worth a lot.
- **Someone with the domain.** The most defensible AI companies are built by people who deeply understand a specific workflow — claims processing, clinical notes, freight — not by generalists.

Write down which of these you actually lack. If you can't say it in one sentence, you're not ready to search yet.

## Where AI co-founders actually come from

In our experience, very few lasting co-founder pairs come from cold outreach or matching platforms. They come from three places.

### People you've already worked with

This is still the best source by a distance. You've seen them under pressure. You know how they handle disagreement. Go through your last three jobs and make a list before you look anywhere else.

### People you've built something with — even something small

The second-best signal is having shipped *anything* together. A hackathon project. A weekend tool. An open-source contribution. This is why our Projects space runs four-week build squads: working on a real thing, with a deadline, tells you more about someone in a month than twenty coffees.

### People in small, trusted rooms

Large communities are good for visibility and bad for trust. Small rooms — where people use real names and have seen each other's thinking over weeks — are where "we should work together" conversations actually start. It's not glamorous. It's just proximity plus time.

## The questions that matter (and the ones that don't)

Skills are the easy part to check. What breaks partnerships is almost always something else. Before you commit, get honest answers to these:

1. **What would make you walk away from this company?** Money running out, a better offer, a family change? Knowing each other's exit conditions early prevents painful surprises.
2. **How much risk can you actually carry?** Months of runway, dependants, visa status. None of it is a dealbreaker; hiding it is.
3. **Who decides when we disagree?** Not in theory — in the specific areas where you'll clash. Product direction. Hiring. Fundraising.
4. **What do you think about AI replacing jobs?** This sounds philosophical, but it isn't. Your product will make choices about who it replaces or empowers, and you'll pitch those choices to customers. Misaligned values here show up in the roadmap within months.
5. **How do you work when you're exhausted?** Everyone is pleasant when rested.

What matters less than people think: identical working hours, being in the same city (though it helps early), and having the same favourite model provider.

## Trial it before you sign it

The best founders we know treat the first six to eight weeks as an explicit trial. A few practical rules:

- **Pick a real, scoped goal.** "Ship a working pilot to two design partners" is better than "explore the idea together."
- **Agree on the trial in writing.** Who owns what if it doesn't work out. Who owns what if it does. Keep it simple — a shared doc is fine at this stage.
- **Have one hard conversation on purpose.** Disagree about something that matters and see how it resolves. If you get through the trial without a single disagreement, you probably weren't being honest with each other.
- **Decide at the end, not gradually.** Set a date. On that date, both of you say yes or no. Drifting into a partnership is how people end up with a co-founder they're not sure about two years in.

## The quiet advantage of doing this in a community

Finding a co-founder alone is lonely and slow. Doing it inside a community of other founders and builders gives you two things you can't get from cold outreach: *references* (someone who's seen this person work) and *repeated exposure* (you see how people think over weeks, not in a single call).

That's the real reason small, invite-only communities produce so many co-founder pairs. Not because of any matching algorithm — but because trust needs time and a room to grow in.
