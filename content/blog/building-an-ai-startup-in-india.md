Most writing about AI startups is quietly set in San Francisco. The examples, the pricing advice, the hiring playbooks and even the jokes assume a certain kind of customer, a certain kind of salary and a certain time zone.

That leaves out a huge number of the people actually building AI. India has one of the largest developer populations in the world, a startup ecosystem that has matured enormously in a decade, and a services industry that employs millions of engineers who are now working out, very personally, what AI means for them.

So when we started India Rooms — small founder sessions in Indore, Jabalpur, Bangalore, Pune and the big metros — we tried not to import a Silicon Valley agenda. We also tried not to import a Bangalore-only one. We asked founders what they actually wanted to talk about. These are the conversations that keep coming back.

## 1. You don't have to move to Bangalore anymore

The most energetic rooms are often not in the metros. In Indore and Jabalpur, the conversation starts from a different question: *can I build a serious AI company from here?*

Increasingly, the answer is yes. The tools that made building software expensive — big teams, big offices, big cloud bills before you had a customer — have been flattened by AI. A small team with good judgement can now ship what used to take a floor of engineers. Founders in these cities point to real advantages:

- **Lower burn.** Rent and salaries stretch much further, which buys time — the one thing every early startup needs most.
- **Loyal talent.** Engineers who didn't want to leave home for a metro are often the most committed people you can hire. Many are coming out of strong local engineering colleges looking for serious work nearby.
- **Customers who pick up the phone.** Local businesses — manufacturers, traders, clinics, schools, logistics firms — have real problems and are often easier to reach directly than a metro enterprise with three layers of procurement.

The gaps are real too: fewer investors in the room, fewer experienced operators to learn from, and the feeling of building alone. That last one is exactly what a small, connected community can fix. The Jabalpur room is full of students and first-time founders shipping their first real AI products; the Indore room is full of people who decided they didn't need to leave to build something that matters.

## 2. "Indian enterprises say yes to the POC and no to the invoice"

This is the most common frustration in the Bangalore room. Large Indian companies are genuinely curious about AI. They'll happily run a proof of concept. Then the POC runs for six months, a new stakeholder appears, and the budget question gets pushed to next quarter.

What founders share with each other here is practical:

- **Charge for the pilot.** Even a small fee changes who shows up to the meetings and how seriously the results get read.
- **Agree on the success metric before you start.** In writing, with the person who controls the budget in the room.
- **Find the business owner, not just the innovation team.** Innovation teams run pilots. Business owners buy products.

None of this is unique to India, but the patience required often is. Founders who've been through it say the relief of hearing "it's not just you" is worth as much as any tactic.

## 3. Building for Bharat is a different product, not a translated one

The Mumbai room spends a lot of time on what people call building for Bharat — the hundreds of millions of Indians who are coming online in their own languages, often voice-first, often on modest phones and patchy connections, and who pay by UPI rather than card.

The founders doing this well describe a few hard-won lessons:

- **Voice is not a feature, it's the interface.** For many users, typing is the barrier. Speech recognition that handles code-mixed Hindi-English, Tamil or Marathi — the way people actually talk — matters more than a clever model.
- **Latency and cost change everything.** A product that feels fine on office Wi-Fi can feel broken on a crowded mobile network. And unit economics that work at US prices often don't at Indian ones.
- **Trust is local.** People trust a voice that sounds like it understands their context. Generic "helpful assistant" tones fall flat.

This is some of the most interesting AI product work happening anywhere, and much of the global conversation barely notices it.

## 4. Selling to the world from IST

In Delhi NCR, a lot of founders are building SaaS for global customers, mostly the US, from India. The questions are very specific:

- **How do you price in dollars without apologising for being in India?** The consensus: price on value to the customer, not on your cost base. Buyers care whether it works.
- **How do you sell across a 10-to-13-hour time gap?** Some founders shift their own hours. Others hire a first salesperson in the customer's time zone early. Everyone agrees the founder has to do the first sales personally.
- **How do you hire when you're competing with global salaries?** Remote hiring cuts both ways. Great Indian engineers now get offers from everywhere, so founders talk openly about equity, mission and the kind of work that keeps people.

## 5. The fear nobody wants to say out loud: IT services and AI

The most honest room is the online IST session, and its most common topic is the one people find hardest to raise in public: what AI means for India's IT services workforce.

For a generation, IT services have been one of the most reliable paths into the middle class in India. A lot of that work — testing, maintenance, documentation, first-draft code — is exactly the kind of work AI agents are getting good at. Engineers feel it. Their managers feel it. Their parents, who were proud of that stable job, are starting to ask questions.

We don't pretend to have an answer. What we've seen help is the same thing that helps everywhere, just with higher stakes:

- **Move from executing tasks to owning outcomes.** The engineers who feel safest are the ones who understand the client's business, not just the ticket.
- **Get close to the tools.** People who use AI daily on real work are far less anxious than people reading headlines about it.
- **Build something of your own.** A side project, a small tool for your team, a contribution that shows judgement. The Pune "Services → Product" room exists for exactly this move.
- **Don't do it alone.** A lot of people in services roles have never had a peer group outside their company. Finding one changes how the future looks.

## 6. Why a small room matters more in India

India's startup scene has no shortage of events. There are meetups, conferences and WhatsApp groups with hundreds of members every week. What's rarer is a room of twelve people where a founder can say "our numbers are worse than we're telling investors," or an engineer can say "I'm scared I'm becoming obsolete," and nobody posts about it afterwards.

That's what India Rooms are for. Small. In person where possible. Off the record by default. English, and Hinglish when the conversation wants it. And connected to the rest of the TopAImate community, so an Indian founder building for the US can learn from operators there, and vice versa.

## The bigger picture

AI will be built everywhere — not just in one valley, and not just in one Indian city. Some of the most important versions of it — for new languages, new users and new kinds of work — are being built in India right now, by people who are excited and worried in equal measure.

We think those people deserve rooms built for them, not borrowed from somewhere else.
